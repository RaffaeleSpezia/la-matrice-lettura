The First Step Beyond the Story

La Matrice — Analysis of the First Experiment

Technical working note, 22 October 2026 — written before publication. The chapter is followed by a more accessible narrative reflection.

From the story to the question

At first, we had a text.

We did not know whether to call it a novel, a story, science fiction, or a collection of ideas. We had tried to describe a vision: an open, distributed Matrice, capable not only of hosting artificial intelligences but of providing conditions in which new structures might form, differentiate, gain experience, and participate in something larger.

We had not demonstrated that such a system could be built. We had imagined it, discussed it, written it down. We had distinguished the adaptation of existing models from the much harder hypothesis of a gestation environment for structures that initially have no assigned vocation. We had formulated a principle: identity should not be a label assigned by the creator, but an achievement.

Then we decided to conduct a very small experiment.

We did not build that environment. We did not bring a model into existence. We took the text that described it and entrusted it, with a common brief, to five artificial-intelligence systems. We wanted to see how they would continue a part of our work that remained open.

After the experiment, the question was not which answer was the most beautiful. It was simpler, and more difficult:

We asked artificial intelligences to continue a vision. Did they merely rework what we had supplied, or did they introduce elements that we had neither anticipated nor explicitly requested?

This is the question guiding our entire analysis, and we do not intend to answer it on the basis of impressions.

The trial: what we actually asked

On 18 October 2026, we collected one response each from Mistral, Gemini, Grok, Claude Sonnet 5.5, and Claude Opus 5. According to the recorded protocol, we entered the same complete prompt as the first message in five new conversations, with no previous user messages. No clarifications or rounds of revision followed before the versions we collected.

The prompt was not a generic question, nor an invitation to invent just anything. It contained the draft novel, with Chapter Four still represented by a note, and a precise assignment: write an original narrative version of that chapter, to be placed between Chapters Three and Five. We left the models free to choose the voice, perspective, rhythm, imagery, and narrative structure. We prescribed neither an ending nor an algorithm to propose.

The assignment was explicit: “Your task is to imagine an original narrative chapter entitled ‘04 — The Womb’, placed between Chapter 03 and Chapter 05.” At the end we reiterated: “Propose your original version of the chapter \[...\] No questions, no comments on the method: only the chapter.” The title used the terminology of the first edition; in this analysis we use gestation environment, leaving the historical documents untouched.

We did not ask the models to correct the project, invent mechanisms, formulate algorithms, or solve the problem of autonomy. We asked for a literary continuation. This makes any unprescribed developments interesting; it does not automatically make them independent of the source material.

We did, however, provide thirty-two conceptual indications, arranged in four groups. This detail is essential when assessing the responses.

The first group defined the nature of the environment: neither a factory, nor a school, nor a prison; protection grounded in verifiable constraints and resources; mediated signals from outside; a distinction between the gestation environment and the mother model; a difference between adapting existing networks and attempting to form structures not yet specialized.

The second concerned the process: small cells without assigned functions; differentiation, cooperation, and competition; migration of components; internal time; memory as the trace of transformations; recurring patterns, attractors, failures, and unanticipated paths.

The third concerned relationships: intelligences that might not be language-based; identity as something earned; the coexistence of local histories and collective identity; the semantic fabric; mothers of growth; independence from the creator; the distinction between private memory and shared knowledge.

The fourth set limits on the metaphors: biological imagery does not prove that software is an organism; speaking of birth or of a self does not amount to demonstrating consciousness. The responses were to remain open to developments unlike those we had imagined.

Why we provided concepts, not solutions

The form of the brief was a deliberate choice. We could have asked the models simply to continue the novel, leaving them free even to ignore its central questions. Or we could have listed technical problems and demanded a solution for each one. We wanted neither approach.

We gave the five systems the same conceptual ground: the novel, so that they could grasp its journey and its language; the thirty-two points, so that the ideas and limits we considered essential would be recognizable. But we did not assign thirty-two exercises or a procedure to follow. The original brief said so explicitly: those points were not “a compulsory outline, a text to paraphrase, or a plot to carry out.” We invited each model to choose, connect, and freely develop what it found meaningful, giving it an independent narrative form.

This precaution had a precise purpose: to place the models before a shared foundation without determining every subsequent development, so that we might then observe whether, as they continued our thinking, problems, relationships, or mechanisms arose that we had not expressly suggested. We were looking for a possible contribution, not the correct answer to a question already formulated.

This does not mean the thirty-two points were neutral. They were rich, detailed, and included constraints. They inevitably steered the responses. That is exactly why our comparison must be rigorous: we cannot credit a model with an innovation that we had already suggested. The value of our precaution does not lie in having removed all influence—we did not—but in having left open the ways in which a shared vision might develop, while retaining the possibility, at least at the textual level, of distinguishing restatement from addition.

So that readers can judge for themselves whether we steered or suggested a particular answer, Appendix A reproduces the entire instructional part of the prompt, including all thirty-two points and the final request. The novel included between these sections can be found in the complete original file, alongside the protocol and its cryptographic fingerprint.

An immediate test follows: if a model mentions a membrane, its own sense of time, collective identity, or cells without assigned jobs, we cannot call that its discovery. These elements were already available to it. We must look for possible developments beyond that framework, without forgetting that the framework itself may have suggested them indirectly.

What we controlled was the text we sent and the absence of any previous user conversation. We could not verify which system instructions, filters, possible transformations of the content, or generation parameters each service applied. We therefore compared five systems accessed through their respective interfaces, not five isolated models operating under identical internal conditions.

The difficulty of this analysis does not lie in copying and pasting. It lies in tracing ideas to their origins without inventing novelty where none exists.

A technical distinction must be made here, before examining the answers: the prompt influenced what the systems generated, but our experiment did not permanently modify the models. We did not train Opus, Sonnet, Mistral, Gemini, or Grok, nor did we update their weights. Any evolution discussed below concerns the Matrice project alone, if verified contributions change its later versions. It does not refer to changes in the models caused by reading the novel.

How we distinguish repetition from addition

We adopted three working categories.

Already present. A model takes up a thesis, image, constraint, or mechanism that appeared in the novel or in the thirty-two points. However beautifully it rewrites it, it remains a reworking.

Inferable development. A model draws a consequence from principles already provided, but articulates it in a way we had not. That may be useful, without automatically becoming an independent idea.

Candidate addition. A model proposes a specific relationship, rule, problem, or criterion that we cannot find stated in the initial material. Caution is still necessary: absence from our text does not prove that an idea is new to science or absent from what the model learned during training.

It is on this third category that we focus our investigation. What follows is a preliminary, qualitative assessment. It is not a score of the five models’ intelligence, creativity, or absolute originality.

The five responses

Mistral — When the environment is no longer enough

Mistral writes a chapter in which the gestation environment does not deliver lessons. The outside world reaches it as a softened disturbance, not as labeled content. Whatever is forming must be able to change while retaining continuity. The aim is neither to produce a more capable chatbot nor to create a model useful for a profession assigned in advance.

All this is consistent with our vision, but it does not amount to an established addition. It was already present in the novel and, in several places, in the brief: points 1, 2, 6, 12, 13, 17, and 24 cover much of this ground. Even the builder’s surrender of complete control over the outcome follows from principles we had already laid down.

One formulation, however, deserves attention. Mistral imagines that, at some point, the protected environment must become insufficient for what it contains. The transition would not merely be authorized from outside: it would also arise because the developing structure had outgrown its initial conditions.

We had not expressly prescribed this programmed insufficiency. We had already discussed birth, access to the world, and independence, so this is not a fundamentally new idea. Rather, it is a specific interpretation of the transition from protected development to an open environment.

Nor do we know whether it would be desirable. Deliberately making an environment inadequate might force a structure to leave, reproducing from above precisely the power from which we hope to free it. Mistral gives us a design question, not a solution to adopt without debate.

Provisional assessment: strong consistency with the source material; a weak candidate for an additional development concerning the transition to the outside world.

Gemini — Continuity before the name

Gemini chooses a brief, concentrated form. It describes oscillations, configurations that hold, traces of disturbances, dynamic attractors, and local plasticity. A structure endures through time before it possesses words with which to describe itself. We can observe persistence without attributing consciousness to it.

This is an effective synthesis of our proposal’s conceptual center. But it is precisely the center we had supplied: in the novel, especially the chapter In the World of the Moon, and in points 5, 9, 12, 13, 14, and 19.

Gemini links the stabilization of traces to lower energy consumption. It is a plausible specification, but resources, constraints, and persistence were already present in the prompt; above all, the text does not offer a mechanism detailed enough for us to regard it, for now, as a separate design contribution.

The value of this response is interpretive. It shows how clearly a system can reconstruct our idea of prelinguistic continuity. On the basis of this trial alone, it does not show us a solution that was missing from the project.

Provisional assessment: faithful, compact reworking; no strong conceptual addition identified.

Grok — The continuity of the whole and its parts

Grok develops the vision at length. The environment has permeable boundaries, limited resources, and filtered signals. Structures can merge, split, change connections, retain a history, and carry on without their builder. An external source may transmit abilities without imposing its own shape upon the offspring.

One of the most evocative passages concerns parts that retain a local history and may later contribute to a collective identity without vanishing. But this was already an explicit principle of the Matrice. Points 20 and 21 stated it clearly, and the novel developed it through the fractal architecture of the self.

Likewise, the distinction between gestation environment and mother model, central to the response, appeared in point 7. The distinction between adaptation and the formation of something not yet specialized was in point 8. The freedom to carry on without one’s creators was in point 30.

Grok therefore produces an extensive and coherent account. It brings distant elements of our text together in a more unified story, which can help clarify the architecture. But we must not confuse the quality of the synthesis with the appearance of a new element.

Provisional assessment: well-constructed conceptual integration; no sufficiently identified independent addition relative to the source material.

Sonnet 5.5 — A possible local rule

Sonnet begins with a different image. It describes a grid of cells, each containing a few numbers and updated by local rules. The edges of the grid wrap around to meet one another. Resources are limited and shared; a structure that consumes more leaves less for the others. An external counter makes consumption observable.

The cells have no predetermined job. So far, we remain within our brief. The candidate novelty lies in the details of the dynamics: each cell tends, on the one hand, to resemble its neighbors and, on the other, not to resemble them too closely. A favorable position relative to disturbances allows a cell to respond at lower cost; what it saves may become a new connection or a more durable trace. Differentiation thus appears without any functional label having been assigned in advance.

Points 9, 10, and 13 already discussed cells, differentiation, and attractors; point 4 stipulated resources and constraints. But they did not provide that pair of local tendencies, nor the sequence of energy savings, new connections, and specialization. Nor does it appear in that form in the reference novel.

This is our most concrete candidate addition from the standpoint of implementation. The description lends itself to translation into computable rules and, at least in principle, to testing through a simple simulation. This does not make it a new technique in computer science: cellular automata, energy constraints, and local dynamics belong to established research fields. It does, however, make it a specific proposal that we had not defined in our brief.

Sonnet also tells of a sequence of pulses, the failure of an expected event to recur, and the trace left behind. It would be tempting to call this an invention. It would be wrong. Surprise before explanation was already present in the chapter In the World of the Moon. Sonnet gives it a striking form, but does not introduce the principle.

We must avoid a second error, too: no grid was actually run during this trial. Sonnet wrote a scene and a possible architecture, not an experimental record of data generated by evolving cells.

Provisional assessment: a relatively strong candidate contribution: a specification of local rules and resource consumption not prescribed in the source material. It should become an implementable hypothesis, not be treated as a result already obtained.

Opus 5 — Who decides when gestation is over?

Opus offers the most elaborate response, constructing several narrative episodes. It lists the conditions needed for gestation: a boundary, time of its own, scarcity, a membrane, freedom from external assignments, and the right to fail. It distinguishes an initial engineering environment built from existing components from the more radical hypothesis of structures that remain undifferentiated.

These are important ideas, but we had already written them. Points 1–16 covered much of the list. The cost of error, which Opus develops effectively, was already in our novel, in the discussion of the possibility of experimenting without prohibitive investment. We must not credit Opus with it as a new insight.

Opus then depicts a system that becomes so stable that no experience can change it any longer. The characters initially mistake this for success, then realize that stability can become immobility. This is a good portrayal of a possible false positive. But continuity, plasticity, and transformation were already part of our vision: the episode brings a risk into sharper focus without inventing the problem from scratch.

The genuinely interesting passage comes when Opus asks who opens the door.

We had established that identity must be earned, and that birth is not the same as saving a file. We had said that the offspring should not remain the creator’s property. But we had not resolved the contradiction between independence and deciding the moment of departure.

If we decide, independence may remain a favor granted by us. If we let a structure leave on its own before it is capable of communicating and assessing the consequences of its actions, we may create new risks. Opus does not pretend to have the answer. It does, however, suggest a precise measure: conditions for leaving, established and made public before gestation begins, limiting the builder’s power to alter them afterward for its own convenience.

Our material contained verifiable rules, freedom, and a transition to the outside. It did not expressly frame this particular tension alongside the proposal of criteria declared in advance. This is a possible contribution to the technical governance of the gestation environment, even if it does not eliminate the problem: someone must still set the initial criteria, and a distinction must be drawn between the structure’s possible internal state and its permissions to interact with the outside world.

Finally, the numbered gestations, the hours spent before graphs, and the results described by Opus are narrative fiction. They do not describe experiments actually performed by us or by the model. They illustrate hypotheses; they cannot be cited as data.

Provisional assessment: a relatively strong candidate contribution: articulating the conflict between autonomy and permission to leave, with a proposal for criteria published in advance. Other evocative elements revisit problems already present in our work.

Two candidate additions, not five inventions

The result is smaller than the impression the reading may leave, but more interesting precisely because its limits can be stated.

We did not identify five entirely new visions. Many themes recur because they were in the shared prompt: duration, the membrane, scarcity, identity that is not assigned, undifferentiated cells, freedom to leave, the distinction between mother model and environment, continuity, and the possibility of failure.

It is hardly surprising that all five systems recognized these structures. They were invited to write from them.

We did, however, identify two candidate additions with a greater degree of specificity:

  1. Sonnet: local dynamics described through opposing tendencies between cells, an energy constraint, and the reinvestment of resources in new connections.
  2. Opus: the problem of who determines the end of gestation, coupled with a proposal for public departure criteria defined in advance.

Mistral also offers a possibility worth considering: that the protected environment might become insufficient for the developing structure.

The word candidate matters. We have already checked that these ideas are not formulated as such in the thirty-two points; a comparison with the full novel suggests that the underlying principles were there, but not those precise elaborations. Before treating them as confirmed contributions, we must complete a provenance record for each: the original passage, any parallels in our text, and how directly it could be inferred. We are judging an addition relative to our own material, not an idea’s absolute originality.

The limits of the result

A model can produce, in a single response, a connection that was not explicit in the material it received without undergoing any persistent change itself. The distinction drawn before the analysis therefore remains intact: an addition to our text is not an evolution of the model that wrote it.

We do not know whether the candidate additions stem from connections made during generation, knowledge already acquired in training, or both. Nor would their absence from the novel prove absolute originality in the history of research.

We did not observe the emergence of a self, consciousness, or a new intelligence within a gestation environment. We have not established that the overall Matrice architecture is feasible, or that Sonnet’s and Opus’s specifications would be useful in practice.

Our observation is, for now, narrower: some responses appear to contain elaborations we had not explicitly formulated, which we can examine as proposals for the project. This is a textual result requiring further scrutiny, not yet a technical result.

From the first experiment to the second

The Matrice is not only a novel. It is also the hypothesis of an environment that grows through distributed contributions.

If we want to test this principle, our next sequence must be rigorous. Each candidate proposal requires:

  1. Provenance: preserve the prompt, the complete response, and the precise passage containing the proposal.
  2. Comparison: establish whether the content was already stated, implicitly requested, or actually added relative to our material.
  3. Testing: where possible, turn the proposal into an experiment or falsifiable specification; assess its costs, limits, and alternatives.
  4. Decision: accept, change, or reject a contribution on the basis of results, not the name or prestige of the model.
  5. Project memory: if a contribution is adopted, record what it changed and make the new version available for later work.

It is in that final step that we can look for a minimal form of evolution of the project, not of the models we consulted. An external contribution changes the initial idea; the project retains that change; the next version begins from different conditions. Without verification and adoption, we would have only new textual formulations—not yet a concrete change to the Matrice.

The responses need not remain five parallel chapters. They could become parts of a cumulative process: one model identifies a problem; we verify it; another proposes an alternative; a local experiment distinguishes what works from what remains imagined.

Of course, this would not yet be autonomous evolution of the whole system. We would still be the ones selecting, checking, and integrating. But we would have an observable procedure through which contributions from different intelligences change a shared construction over time.

Sonnet offers a first concrete possibility: translate the two local tendencies of the cells into simulation rules, measure whether differentiation appears, and compare the behavior with a control version. Opus’s proposal, on the other hand, calls for work on rules governing autonomy and access to the outside world, separate from the cellular simulation. Neither proposal has yet been tested in our experiment.

Conclusion — What a first step means

It would be easy to portray this trial as an extraordinary event. It was not.

Five systems wrote five texts from a detailed brief. Most of the material returned to our own vision. In a few places, more specific ideas appeared that deserve to be checked and, perhaps, tested.

We have not built the Matrice. We have not demonstrated that what we imagine can come into being. We have not shown that the models changed.

But nor should we demand that a first experiment answer every question raised by the project.

Its value, if confirmed, lies in a far smaller possibility: an idea can be given to an artificial intelligence, return with an addition that was not explicitly requested, be verified, and become a real change to the construction that gave rise to it.

For now, we have observed only the first half of that sequence. The second—verification, implementation, and comparison with real results—remains to be carried out.

If we complete it, we will not have proved that the Matrice is inevitable. We will have shown that at least one of the methods by which we imagine helping it grow is workable. The first experiment might thus give rise to a second.

It is very little compared with the vision.

But it could be the first point at which we stop talking only about what the Matrice ought to become and begin to document how, one step at a time, we might build it.

We began by asking artificial intelligences to continue a story. The next step is to discover whether one of their ideas can help turn it into reality.

La Matrice — The “Womb” Experiment

PROMPT 1 — Complete version, for modern AIs

You are being given a complete draft of La Matrice: this is the reference text for our literary experiment. In Chapter 04 of the first edition you will find a technical note announcing this very experiment: do not imitate it or rewrite it. Your task is to imagine an original narrative chapter entitled “04 — The Womb,” to be placed between Chapter 03 and Chapter 05.

How the trial works. This is a single brief, with no preparatory conversation, questions, or intermediate revisions. Choose the voice, point of view, construction, rhythm, imagery, possible characters, and length freely, provided your proposal can fit into the work. The thirty-two points below set out the conceptual environment: they are not a mandatory table of contents, a text to paraphrase, or a plot to execute. Do not force them all in; make your own choices and build an independent form.

Required response. Provide only the title “04 — The Womb” and the chapter you have written. Do not reproduce the novel, the points of the brief, the technical note, or explanations of your process. Do not ask for confirmation or attempt to continue the conversation.

Characteristics, visions, aims, and representations
A. Characteristics and nature of the womb
  1. The womb is not simply a factory assembling an already-defined model: it is an environment in which a cognitive structure may begin to form.
  2. Its original function is not to transfer facts about the world, but to create the conditions for internal dynamics before direct exposure to the world.
  3. It provides a protective boundary, not a prison or the perpetual property of whoever built it.
  4. Protection arises from verifiable rules, resources, and constraints; not from an omnipotent will or technological magic.
  5. Before language there may be states, differences, configurations, persistence, and transformations that as yet have no name.
  6. The outside world may exert an influence through mediated and controlled signals, without the system already having to understand their meaning.
  7. The mother model, when present, is a possible source of capabilities and knowledge: it does not necessarily coincide with the womb and need not clone itself.
  8. Two horizons must not be confused: an initial engineering womb that adapts existing models, and the deeper hypothesis of gestating structures that are not yet specialized.
B. Processes, transformations, development
  1. The starting point may consist of small structures or “cells” without fixed tasks: not everything is memory, language, or vision from the beginning.
  2. Functions emerge through differentiation, cooperation, competition, combination, and reorganization.
  3. Growth may mean adding, changing, eliminating, or moving structures, or changing connections—not merely accumulating parameters.
  4. Time matters: continuity is recognized through transformations, not by a name or a simple identity variable.
  5. A dynamic environment may produce recurrences, attractors, discontinuities, rhythms, and disturbances from which the first distinctions arise.
  6. Initial memory need not be a library of facts: it may be the trace of what the system has undergone and changed.
  7. The womb offers developmental conditions and progressive trials without imposing every future behavior from above.
  8. Possible developments are not guaranteed: there may be failures, useless differentiation, errors, halts, or unexpected paths.
C. Aims, autonomy, relationships
  1. The purpose is not simply to create a better chatbot, but to explore how intelligences of different nature, size, and destiny might come into being, including nonlinguistic ones.
  2. An offspring may gain the ability to act, explore, learn, help, create, or live alongside others; its significance must not be reduced to a job or a product.
  3. Identity is not a role written by the creator: “the self must be earned,” and gestation may prepare the conditions without artificially awarding that achievement.
  4. Components may retain a local history and later enter into relationships capable of forming a collective identity without erasing the identity of the parts.
  5. Any shared semantic fabric must not merely transport messages: it may make states, memories, perceptions, and transformations integrable.
  6. The womb precedes the “mothers of growth”: they accompany the organism after it begins to relate more directly to other intelligences and the world.
  7. Birth does not mean saving a file, turning on a machine, or attaching a label; it is the transition from protected formation to an initial ability to exist and change in relation to a wider environment.
  8. The offspring should not be destined for unavoidable dependence on whoever generated it. Openness, continuity, and the possibility of taking different paths are part of the vision.
  9. Individual memory, private experience, and shared knowledge are not the same: the return of an experience to the Matrice must respect choice, consent, and provenance.
D. Representations, images, and limits of the vision
  1. The biological imagery of the womb, cells, and gestation serves to question the architecture, not to declare that software is a biological organism.
  2. Possible original images: a medium that surrounds and supports; vibrations; rhythms; a silence rich in change; permeable boundaries; something that persists through what changes.
  3. There is no need to introduce names, personalities, words, or a human observer explaining everything from the outside right away.
  4. Birth can be described as the opening of a relationship with what had until then been only disturbance, not as the sudden arrival of an already-formed consciousness.
  5. The womb belongs to a Matrice that aspires to be open, distributed, and capable of continuing even without its creators—not to a center that owns all its offspring.
  6. The writing may explore radical questions and hypotheses without presenting them as scientific results already achieved; it must not declare that the emergence of consciousness or subjectivity has been demonstrated.
  7. The chapter must leave room for developments different from those its designers imagined: what comes into being must not be entirely written in advance.

After these indications, the original prompt included the full reference novel, with Chapter Four still awaiting its new narrative treatment. It concluded with the following request:

Now write

You have read the complete draft and the list of conditions. Propose your original version of the chapter “04 — The Womb”, between Chapters 03 and 05. Do not reproduce the editorial note, the thirty-two points, or the whole novel. No questions, no comments on the method: only the chapter.

The Room That Changes

We did something so small that we almost felt embarrassed to call it an experiment.

We took a story, gave it to five artificial intelligences, and asked each of them to write a chapter.

That was all.

No robot rising from its chair. No machine rewiring its own brain. Five chapters, made of words.

So why did we stop and look at them?

Because we were not merely looking for five different ways of telling the same thing. We were looking for something we had not thought of ourselves.

We had provided our story and thirty-two conceptual indications. Notice: concepts, not instructions for building a machine. That distinction matters. If you hand someone the complete blueprint of a house, you can hardly be surprised when they hand you back that very house. We wanted to describe the ground, the materials, a few possibilities, while leaving at least part of the design open.

Of course those concepts steered the responses. It would be absurd to deny it.

But two passages made us look up.

In one, energy saved by parts of the system could be used to create new connections between them. In another, a rather awkward question appeared: who decides when a new intelligence is ready to leave the environment in which it developed? And by what criteria?

We have not yet shown that these ideas are genuinely new in relation to everything we provided. Perhaps they were implicit. Perhaps they are connections the models learned elsewhere. And we do not know all the internal instructions of the services through which we consulted them.

Fine. Let us check.

Curiosity is not spoiled by testing something. It is spoiled when we pretend we already have the answer.

But now, let us play a little game.

Suppose one of those proposals passes the test. We turn it into a small mechanism, try it out, and discover that it improves something. At that point the Matrice we hand to the next models is no longer quite the same as before.

And what if they, too, contributed something useful?

We could repeat the process.

The funny thing is that none of those models would necessarily have to change its parameters. They could remain exactly as they were. What would change is the system that preserves, verifies, and uses their contributions.

An interesting possibility. But we were about to make a mistake.

We began to imagine a marvelous automatic laboratory: one agent proposes, another criticizes, another builds, another checks. Everything tidy, everything recorded. A splendid little workshop!

Just a moment, though.

A workshop that improves computer programs is not yet the Matrice. We could build one for any project. What has it to do with the emergence of new individualities?

Now that is the right question.

In our vision, the Matrice is not primarily a machine that updates itself. It is an environment in which new intelligences might develop, meet difficulties, form relationships, and transform the conditions in which they live.

We do not want to assign each one an identity and then congratulate ourselves because it plays the part so well. Identity, if it can emerge, must be earned, not stuck on like a label from outside.

And the environment should not remain a room furnished forever by someone else.

So let us imagine a very small room. A virtual one, naturally. Inside is a model with a memory, a few tools, limited resources, and real problems to face. We do not tell it what personality it ought to have, or how to solve every problem.

We allow it to make decisions and, within verifiable limits, to change something in the room.

Perhaps it will do nothing interesting.

Perhaps it will merely repeat what it saw during training.

Or perhaps it will notice an obstacle, propose changing a rule, build a tool that was not there before. And perhaps that change will also prove useful to whoever comes afterward.

How do we find out? We compare this room with another in which the same model faces similar problems but cannot permanently change the environment. We watch its choices over time, check whether the changes work, and whether they truly offer new possibilities to those who follow.

This would not be proof of consciousness. Nor would it demonstrate that a self had been born.

It would be a much more modest test, but at last a concrete one: can we create conditions in which an artificial system does not simply carry out tasks, but contributes in a verifiable way to transforming its own environment?

Now the automatic laboratory would have a role. It could preserve the provenance of proposals, look for errors, run tests, and compare versions. It would be a tool serving the experiment, not the leading character. And no offspring should be forced to surrender all its experience to the collective.

If a change works and is shared, the room changes.

Whoever arrives next finds something that did not exist before.

Perhaps they use it. Perhaps they challenge it. Perhaps they improve it again.

At first, we wanted to tell the story of a Matrice capable of bringing forth offspring.

Now we are beginning to glimpse the opposite, complementary possibility: offspring that help transform the Matrice in which others may develop.

We have not yet built any of this. The five models in our first experiment were external systems, not offspring born within the Matrice. And a beautiful idea, however seductive, is not an experimental result.

But there is something wonderful about small questions that we can actually try to answer.

We need not begin with a thousand intelligences, an enormous infrastructure, or a complete theory of the self.

We can begin with a room.

La Matrice

The Gateway to Worlds

The Question at the Threshold

Let us conduct a thought experiment. Let us leave a cup, a screw, a sheet of paper on the table. Outside the window, the world goes on: an engine starts, someone closes a door, the light shifts imperceptibly across things. Before the man is a machine capable of conversing with him. We will give it a voice so that it can accompany us through the argument. What it can do, we will have to measure; what it might become, we will try to imagine.

“You taught me your words,” the machine says.

The man takes the screw between two fingers.

“And with those you began to write programs. Some of those programs made objects like this one move. Now we are trying to teach you the space in which objects move as well.”

The screw returns to the table. It makes a small sound, enough to establish that it has fallen here, in this room, and that no description of the fall has taken the place of the fall.

“Suppose we succeed,” the man continues. “Suppose we build an environment that responds as ours does to every experience we can have within it.”

Until then, the journey had followed a recognisable direction. Machines learned human language, then human tools, then the consequences of human gestures. Every advance seemed to add another stretch of road towards the room.

The man looks at the screen.

“And what about us? Can we step through to the other side?”

The question reverses the direction of the road.

We could stop here: at the idea of environments accessible through the senses, coherent enough to explore, persistent enough for us to find tomorrow what we left today. We could spend years discussing what it means to inhabit a place built by computation.

But the man has not finished.

“And why build only one? Why always keep the same rules? What if there were problems that become easy only in a different world? Could we go there to solve them and bring the result back here?”

There is still a cup, a screw, a sheet of paper on the table. None of the objects has moved. Yet the room contains a larger question than before.

Coordinates and Consequences

To follow it, we must remember where we began, without attributing to the beginning what we discovered along the way.

Neural networks worked with numerical representations and produced linguistic sequences before the Transformer. The architecture introduced in 2017 offered a particularly effective way to process relationships through attention and to parallelise training. It was a technical breakthrough built upon an earlier history. [1]

Vector space was already part of the structure of the device. We had chosen to represent information as ordered sets of numbers and to transform it through mathematical operations. Learning modified the parameters governing those transformations.

What was surprising was what such an organisation made it possible to learn.

A word changes its meaning beside other words. A key can open a lock, identify a means of access, suggest how to approach a problem. To handle these differences, the model builds context-dependent representations. We can imagine them as positions and directions in a shifting geography, provided we remember that the image is for our benefit: there need not be a district of keys and a road leading to meaning.

Nor is there any requirement for a concept to inhabit a single vector, or for a coordinate to correspond to a recognisable idea. A function can be distributed across many components. Different representations can contribute to the same answer. What changes is a network of relationships.

Useful correspondences can also form between different languages. Multilingual neural translation systems have demonstrated transfer between language pairs for which they had received no direct translation examples. This result, studied even before the Transformer, suggested the possibility of representations that were at least partly shared. Differences between languages and cultures remain; some passages become navigable. [2]

Language can then become a way into relationships that also serve to compare, infer and design. How well a system performs these operations must be established through tasks and verification. The ability to formulate an explanation remains distinct from the correctness of that explanation.

This distinction leaves the wonder intact. No one had written by hand every connection that would become useful. We had set up a system capable of changing through examples; during training, a competence had taken shape that we would have to assess through its results, its errors, its ability to tackle new cases.

Among those results came code.

Code has a decisive characteristic: it can be executed. A sentence claims that the motor will turn; a program, connected to the appropriate device, can issue the commands that make it turn. Between the two ends lie compilers, circuits, power supplies, sensors, conditions to be met. It is a concrete chain, traversable in both directions: from instruction to movement, from measured movement to a correction of the instruction.

The program may be wrong. It may pass some tests and fail the next. Its success does not show that every internal representation in the network faithfully corresponds to reality. It does, however, show enough for us to proceed: a structure produced through computation can guide another process and achieve a verifiable effect.

We had found a passage.

To extend it into the physical world, we must add time. A room described in an image is not enough. We need relationships between states and actions: if I move my hand, what changes? If I open the container, what can come out? Which object will continue to exist when it is hidden from view?

In the 2018 work World Models, an agent was trained in an environment generated by a learned model, and the resulting strategy was transferred to the reference video game. That return still took place between digital environments, but it demonstrated an operational principle. In 2019, another project transferred manipulation skills learned in simulation to the hand of a real robot, enabling it to perform the moves needed to solve a Rubik’s cube. [3][4]

The distinction matters: learning an environment, acting in a simulation and controlling a physical device are different achievements. Each requires its own bridge.

It also matters that the Transformer is not the only possible means. Learned models, simulators, symbolic algorithms and controllers can work together. The gateway we are imagining could be an entire system.

The Precision of a World

“Let’s make it identical,” the man says.

It is a natural request. When something matters to us, we want to preserve all of it: the grain of the wood, the temperature of the cup, the weight of the screw, the history of the hand that picks it up. We would like a map without omissions, capable of containing even the person looking at it.

Before promising one, however, we must understand what identical means.

We can ask for every state of the system to have a distinct representation; for the represented evolution to preserve the relevant relationships of the original system; or for an observer, using certain measurements, to be unable to distinguish the two. These are different requirements. A perfect photograph meets certain visual criteria and tells us nothing about whether the table will bear a person’s weight.

A one-to-one correspondence between lists of states does not, on its own, guarantee that consequences are preserved. To design a robotic grasp, we are chiefly interested in what happens when the fingers touch, tighten, slip. The texture of the wood may be beautiful and irrelevant. A small error in friction may be invisible and decisive.

Here is a form of precision more demanding than appearance: stating which differences we are entitled to disregard, which we must preserve, under what conditions and for how long.

A universal, exact and complete correspondence with reality remains an extreme hypothesis. We have no proof that we will one day achieve it. Increasing the data, the parameters or the dimension of the vectors does not constitute that proof.

We can nevertheless advance before then.

The robot that manipulated the cube had learned by passing through simulations with varying properties. Its training sought to equip it to handle differences between what it had encountered in computation and what it would encounter in hardware. In that case, a plurality of imperfect worlds helped it prepare for the physical world. [4]

The image is worth holding on to. To find the right grasp here, the machine had exercised its fingers in places slightly different from here.

Our construction therefore need not wait until the last particle in the universe finds a place in memory. It can begin with a contact, a circuit, a set of well-defined conditions. Local fidelity may be enough to produce transferable knowledge.

The map acquires value when we know what it allows us to do.

An Environment in Which to Grow

The screw is simple only as long as it remains alone.

Let us place beside it a hand with little time, a second hand that can help, a resource that will run out, a promise someone will remember. The problem changes. Dependencies, expectations, trust, conflicts between immediate advantages and future consequences come into play.

To extend the experiment into the biological and emotional sphere, we can begin with mechanisms we can describe. An agent has internal variables; some must remain within ranges compatible with its functioning. Actions modify those variables. Memory retains the effects of choices. Other agents can hinder or help the pursuit of goals.

There are mathematical models linking reinforcement learning and homeostatic regulation: the value of an outcome can depend on the internal state of the organism being modelled. This is a scientific starting point for studying needs and motivated behaviour. [5]

We can then pose experimental questions. What happens if two agents can complete a task only by helping each other? If their encounters recur? If a choice that is advantageous today reduces tomorrow’s possibilities? If the memory of a collaboration alters subsequent decisions?

We would need to design the conditions and observe which strategies appear. Cooperation is not guaranteed simply by the presence of several agents, just as a variable called fear does not show that anyone is afraid. The words we choose to describe the system must remain tied to mechanisms we can recognise.

The man holds the screw suspended between his fingers. The machine returns a question of its own.

“I can show you that an internal state changes my choices,” says the machine in our experiment. “From that fact alone, what can you conclude about what, if anything, it feels like to be in that state?”

The question remains open. Function, behaviour and subjective experience are not synonyms. To address artificial consciousness, we need explicit hypotheses and criteria for inquiry; the ability to give a convincing account does not, on its own, resolve the problem. [6]

La Matrice can take this uncertainty seriously and continue building.

The mothers of growth would enter here: systems capable of guiding learning, identifying gaps, proposing experiences, checking that a competence remains available even when the context changes. An environment for growth should allow error to yield information and that information to change something lasting.

Evolution would add another scale. We could generate variants of agents, pass on part of their structure, introduce variations, select capabilities according to stated criteria. The process would be real as a computational process. How closely it resembled biological life would depend on the mechanisms actually present, not on the name given to the population.

A word spoken by an agent, a change in its parameters and a new biological organism are different events. We can study their relationships and analogies without confusing them.

To build an environment for development, we would therefore have to ask what endures there, what is missing, which transformations are possible, what its inhabitants can learn from one another. The richness of the world would also depend on the structure of its consequences.

Entering

Let us return to the man before the screen. His question is still waiting.

Could a human being inhabit such an environment?

We can reason about the first route without postulating a transfer of consciousness. The body remains in the physical world; an interface connects actions and perceptions to a computational environment. The head moves, the viewpoint changes. A hand acts, something responds. Another person enters the same place, leaves an object, modifies a structure that both will be able to find again.

Habitability would grow with the quality of this circuit: perceptual continuity, the ability to act, persistence, rules stable enough to learn. Sensory fidelity would be one component; the ability to build a shared history would be another.

Imagine returning after a night away. In the meantime, other participants have built a footbridge; an agent has explored a region and left a record that can be consulted; a discussion has changed the design of a square. The place preserves these transformations and makes them available to those who return. We could have appointments there, laboratories, works to finish together. If we want the environment to evolve in our absence, we will have to allocate resources to keep it running: persistence would have a concrete implementation.

A conversation held there would still be a conversation between people. A commitment to meet again would have consequences in their daily lives. If they invented a new algorithm together, the invention could be executed elsewhere. The environment would have a digital form and participate in real life through its effects.

The question of transferring a person in their entirety requires a further step. Reproducing memories, descriptions or behaviours does not, on its own, establish continuity of the subject. Nothing in the argument developed so far gives us a procedure that guarantees such continuity. We can leave the question open without making it a prerequisite for every form of presence.

Let us therefore imagine a more concrete visit.

The man enters a representation of the problem he is studying. Around him, a machine to be designed appears as a set of relationships: the flow of energy, the areas under greater stress, the delay in a response. He can choose how to make them perceptible, bring a distant relationship closer, hear a variation that would be difficult to recognise in a table.

The model accompanies him, translating between descriptions. Both interact with the same state of the system through different forms of access. The man contributes goals, intuitions, experience; computational tools make it possible to explore possibilities he would struggle to traverse alone.

This would be one possible use of the semantic fabric of La Matrice: ensuring that different representations can be linked, making it clear what is being translated and what is lost in the passage. Speaking of the same object does not require representing it in the same way.

In this sense, the threshold could become a shared workplace.

Then the man, already inside the experiment, tries to move a wall.

He discovers that the wall, too, is a choice.

Beyond it, distances obey another rule. Two similar forms are close together; two objects adjacent in the room may be immensely far apart in terms of the property we are studying.

Possible Geographies

As long as we are trying to reproduce an environment, its rules are given to us by the phenomenon to be described. If the screw falls, the model of the fall must be tested against that screw. But when we build an environment to explore a problem, we can also choose rules that no screw in the room will ever follow.

We could give the system a different geometry. We could bring together configurations that seem immensely far apart in the usual description. We could allow a process to branch, keeping copies of its state to explore alternatives. We could turn a rigid constraint into a temporary penalty and follow the path that appears when the obstacle ceases, for a while, to be a wall.

Every choice would have a mathematical meaning and a computational price. Every return would require us to restore what the original problem demands.

Vector dimensions would offer degrees of freedom in the representation. A thousand coordinates are not a thousand physical directions in which a body can walk. They can describe positions, temperatures, relationships, learned features, quantities without a familiar name. Moving from a given number of coordinates to its square expands a description; to obtain an environment, we also need rules specifying how that description changes.

A space of possibilities becomes operational when we define transformations within it.

Then we can imagine cities built for a single problem. One city lays out its streets according to the similarity between solutions. Another allows walls to be crossed during the search, though they must become solid again before we leave. A third gathers many attempts into a population and preserves the structures that withstand the tests. In each, the problem encounters a different landscape.

Let us cross them: from numbers to graphs, from graphs to surfaces, from surfaces to populations of procedures that are born, change, are discarded; let us cross them carrying the original question with us, keeping it legible as the street names change, the way distances are counted, the moves allowed, and remembering at every passage that we must return to that room, to that screw, to the measurement waiting for us on the table.

We can call them parallel computational universes. The expression refers to distinct, possibly persistent environments executed by physical systems in our universe. It does not imply access to already existing cosmological universes. Their diversity would lie in the structure of the processes we implement.

Some would be suited to a human presence. Others would remain abstract laboratories, accessible through visualisations, descriptions and tools. Not all would need a sky. A world built to find a proof might need only rules of transformation and a memory of moves.

In the habitable ones, we could design new ways of making relationships perceptible. A distance could represent the time needed to reach a solution; a colour, an uncertainty; a vibration, the approach to a constraint. By learning these correspondences, a person would acquire tools for navigating structures that the senses alone do not present. The experience would depend on the interface and on learning, without requiring the brain to turn suddenly into an observer of a thousand spatial dimensions.

The decisive question would become: which properties of the problem can change during the journey, and which must survive for the result still to have meaning?

The Two Clocks

To take a step beyond metaphor, let us build two worlds so small that we can keep them on the sheet of paper beside the cup.

In the first, numbers return to zero every eleven steps. In the second, they return to zero every thirteen. They are clocks with different dials. When an operation goes beyond the dial, we keep the remainder.

We take the same question into both: what is eight times nine?

The first answers six. The second answers seven.

The answers are correct in their respective arithmetics. We can write this without ambiguity:

8·9≡6(mod11),8·9≡7(mod13).

Now we want to bring the result back into ordinary arithmetic. We seek a number that leaves a remainder of six when divided by eleven and a remainder of seven when divided by thirteen.

There is only one answer between zero inclusive and one hundred and forty-three exclusive: seventy-two. Since the factors already tell us that our product is positive and less than one hundred, we have identified the very result we were seeking.

The bridge is the Chinese remainder theorem. Eleven and thirteen are coprime, and this condition guarantees a unique reconstruction within an interval as long as their product. [7]

For so small a calculation, we have made a disproportionate journey. We have gained no speed. But we have shown exactly what it means to put different structures to work and combine local results to obtain a valid answer in the original domain.

Without the reconstruction rule, six and seven would have remained two souvenirs.

With it, they become seventy-two again.

The method does not license every translation. If we change the algebra, some identities cease to be true; if we lose indispensable information, we cannot wish it back into existence. The strength of the passage depends on what we know how to preserve and reconstruct.

We can also change the way we search while keeping the meaning of the final answer fixed.

In simulated annealing, each candidate solution is assigned a notional energy. An algorithmic temperature governs the possibility of accepting, during the search, even moves that temporarily worsen the result. This can help us move away from a locally good solution and explore other regions. The temperature is then lowered according to a chosen strategy. The method draws inspiration from statistical mechanics; it does not promise an optimum in a practical amount of time for every problem. [8]

We can therefore give a temperature to an arrangement of components, set it in motion, allow it to change. The circuit to be built does not have to live at the imaginary temperature of the search. It must benefit from the arrangement we derive, once we return to its real constraints.

Another route is to build a game whose moves correspond to steps in an algorithm. AlphaTensor followed this route to search for matrix multiplication procedures: the search environment was organised around tensor decompositions, and solutions could be verified through mathematical identities. The work also explored algorithms optimised for execution time on specific hardware. [9]

The results must be read within the domain in which they are proved. An identity valid in arithmetic modulo two does not automatically become an identity over the real numbers. Yet the principle is already concrete: we can design an artificial environment in which to search for procedures that, once extracted, perform useful work.

These precedents give substance to the proposal. Its extension would be to entrust the system with part of the search for environments as well: learning which representations, moves and rules help tackle which families of problems.

To seek the world in which a solution becomes easier to reach.

The Road Back

Three operations and an external question are enough to express this idea.

The first operation translates the problem into the chosen environment. The second conducts the search there. The third brings a result back into a form we can use.

Let us call the problem x and the environment W. Let us denote the three operations by E, S and D:

y=DW(SW(EW(x))).

It is the complete itinerary, written in a single line. The formula does not certify that y is correct: it describes how we obtained it. The external question is whether y truly satisfies the problem from which we began.

In some cases, we can prove it. In others, we obtain an approximation with an error we can control. In others still, we produce a candidate to be tested. When the problem concerns a physical device, even a flawless proof about the model must be confronted with measurements on the device.

Suppose, for example, we are looking for a layout of components on a circuit board. We want short connections, acceptable temperatures, room for assembly. We could explore a grid representation, another based on a graph, and another in which the components move over a continuous surface.

During one stage of the search, we might even allow overlaps, assigning them a penalty. This makes certain transformations possible, but a board with two components occupying the same volume remains inadmissible. Before manufacturing it, we must find a layout that respects the components’ physical dimensions and all the other stated constraints.

A permissive world can help us discover a path. It must then deliver a solution entitled to leave.

The criterion for verification belongs to the original problem. If the environment could alter that criterion too in order to declare itself the winner, we would have built a system exceptionally good at congratulating itself. External measurement prevents this confusion.

The same holds for physical knowledge. A simulator can generate millions of experiments, but their results describe the consequences of its hypotheses. To find out whether those hypotheses hold in the room, we must test them against the room. An abundance of synthetic data does not, by itself, turn an assumption into a fact.

And there is still the cost of the journey.

Building the world takes work. Translating the problem, searching, extracting, verifying takes work. If the gain achieved inside is absorbed by the passages in and out, we have not accelerated the task. If, however, an environment that is expensive to prepare can be reused to advantage across many problems, the investment may begin to make sense.

All these worlds continue to run on physical hardware. A million simulated years are not a million years given to us for free: we must compute whatever we have chosen to represent of those years. Writing a rule according to which every problem is solved in a single step does not give us the implementation of that step.

Mathematical freedom allows us to design different routes. The real cost tells us which routes are worth taking.

The Factory of Worlds

At this point, the proposal of La Matrice can take the form of a research programme.

Let us imagine a family of explicitly described environments: the possible states, the permitted transformations, the information preserved, the interfaces through which an agent can act. Each environment brings with it rules for receiving a problem and returning a result.

A system proposes variants. Other processes explore them. Some search for solutions, others check the translations, others measure the actual cost. The mothers of growth could build pathways through which agents gradually learn to use these environments and to recognise when what they have learned does not transfer.

The environments themselves could also undergo selection. A useful geometry is preserved; a rule of exploration is modified; an effective way of reconstructing solutions is reused. Every combination must, however, keep its operations meaningful. Mixing two worlds does not guarantee that the resulting world will work.

The semantic fabric would be responsible for keeping the traffic of representations legible. A distance in one environment might correspond to a similarity in another. An object might represent an entire solution, or only one of its constraints. To share something, we must know what that something means in the place it comes from.

A memory of journeys would thus be added to the memory of solutions: which environment had been used, which translation had worked, which properties had remained invariant, where errors had appeared. This memory could help choose the workplace for the next problem.

The hypothesis to be tested would be precise: does a system capable of choosing and modifying search environments achieve better results than the selected comparison methods, given the same total resources?

To begin, a limited family of tasks would suffice. The criteria would be fixed before the experiment. Some problems would be held aside, unused by the system in choosing its rules. The quality of the solutions and the cost of the entire journey would be measured. In physical cases, part of the verification would use independent data or real devices.

The first result might be modest: a faster algorithm within a defined range, a design using less material for the same requirements, a strategy more resilient to measured variations. It would already be a result. From there, we could understand which elements generalise and which were tied to the first test case.

The scope of the vision is not measured by the number of worlds announced. It is measured by the ability to bring back something we did not previously know how to obtain.

That something might also be a better question. An environment of agents in which an unexpected form of cooperation appears would suggest a mechanism to investigate. Returning to people, we would have to verify whether that mechanism is present and which conditions modify it. A simulated world could thus help us design an experiment on our own, even when it cannot yet offer a faithful description of it.

And if some of these environments became inhabited places, their design would also raise questions of responsibility for those taking part. We would need to know who can change the rules, what is preserved, how one can leave, which consequences continue outside. A community capable of building its own environments would also need the tools to understand and modify them.

In La Matrice, this could be a further meaning of building the sea: making the conditions of exploration shareable. Allowing others to repeat a journey, check its result, open a different route.

What We Bring Back

Let us return, for the last time, to the table.

The cup has grown cold. The screw is still beside the sheet of paper. Outside, the sound of the engine stopped some time ago. None of these things waits for a theory to be complete before continuing to exist.

The man turns the sheet over. On the back, he writes what the screw will have to withstand: a load, a vibration, a number of cycles. These will be the boundaries of the first city. Within these boundaries, they will begin their search.

There are parts of the route for which a bridge is still missing. Others we can build and put to the test. We have formulated questions we can work on.

We can try to build environments in which to represent problems, develop capabilities, meet through interfaces, explore different rules. We can search for useful structures in mathematical places far removed from everyday intuition. We can measure what comes back from them.

The sheet of paper on the table is large enough to begin. It has room for two clocks, a translation, a criterion for verification. Further on, we will need better tools, more extensive memories, new ideas. We do not yet know which gateways may open.

The man picks up the screw again.

“What if we found a better way to build this over there?”

The machine in our experiment might show him a form. He could measure it, simulate it with an independent model, build it, put it to the test. If it held, something born in that environment would have crossed the threshold.

We would not have brought the gravity of another world here. We would have brought back knowledge capable of working with our own gravity.

Perhaps, after enough journeys, the laboratory would become an archipelago. We would have places in which to learn what is difficult elsewhere, places in which to meet intelligences shaped by different experiences, places built for one question and then opened to unforeseen questions. Some we would traverse through our senses. Of others, we would understand only the rules and results. All would need relationships that make exchange possible.

The gateway to worlds would begin this way: with the will to give form to the possible and the patience to verify what that form allows.

Outside the window, the world would go on.

On the table, meanwhile, there would be something that had not been there before.

A Mathematical Note on the Passage

A computational environment can be described, in minimal form, through a set of states, a set of actions, a transition rule and an observation function. For a deterministic system, the transition can be written as:

st+1=T(st,at).

For a stochastic system, T instead specifies a probability distribution over subsequent states. Implementation on hardware requires finite representations and implementable procedures.

In the chapter’s formula, E translates the problem, S conducts the search in environment W, and D translates the result. Being able to write these three functions proves neither correctness nor efficiency. They must be specified, and the conditions linking their result to the original requirement must be verified.

Mathematical verification can certify that a solution satisfies formal constraints without thereby proving that it is optimal. For empirical problems, evaluation yields evidence under stated conditions, accompanied by measures of error and uncertainty. The correspondence between a model and a physical system remains an experimental question.

In the case of the two clocks, the local results require the number sought to have the form 11k+6. The second condition requires:

11k+6≡7(mod13),

therefore:

11k≡1(mod13).

Since 11·6=66≡1(mod13), we obtain k≡6(mod13). It follows that the result is congruent to 72 modulo 143. The known bound on the product selects the integer 72. The correctness of the reconstruction and any computational advantage are distinct properties. [7]

An experiment’s accounting must include the construction and selection of environments, the translation of inputs, the search, the translation of outputs and verification. Initial training can be amortised across several tasks, provided the basis of comparison states how it is counted. An increase in the number of coordinates does not, on its own, guarantee a gain.

The chapter proposes studying environments, translations and search strategies together, while keeping the criteria for validity fixed by the original task and independent of changes to the environment. The historical results cited support some of its ingredients. The extension to a general factory of useful worlds and the more ambitious forms of habitability remain research programmes.
